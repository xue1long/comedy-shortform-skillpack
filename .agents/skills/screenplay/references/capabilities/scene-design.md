# Scene Design

## R — Reading
Field's atomic unit: a scene is a unit of action with a want and an obstacle. "All drama is conflict" — without conflict there is no action; without action, no character; without character, no story. A scene exists only where a character's want collides with a concrete obstacle.

## I — Interpretation
Scene = unit of conflict. The "conflict" is structural (want vs obstacle), not emotional (shouting). A scene where nothing is contested — two people agreeing, or one person monologuing — is not a scene, it's exposition. Field's diagnostic: if you can't name the want and the obstacle, the scene fails.

## A1 — Application
For shortform comedy: every beat must have a want-obstacle pair. The comic version — trivial want pursued at disproportionate effort — still requires a real obstacle or the comedy is empty. Check each beat: "who wants what, and what stops them?"

## A2 — Trigger signals
- "Write a scene"
- "This scene is boring / flat"
- "What's the conflict in this scene?"
- "Structure this scene"

## E — Executable steps
1. State the character's want in one sentence (specific, immediate).
2. State the obstacle — concrete, not abstract.
3. Confirm collision: does the want directly hit the obstacle?
4. Identify what changes at the scene's end (the turn).
5. Check: is the turn driven by the collision, or by coincidence?

## B — Boundary / common failures
- **Trap**: want without obstacle — the character just does things.
- **Trap**: obstacle without want — events happen to the character.
- **Trap**: scene ends where it started — no turn, no scene.
- **Not** for sketch comedy's rule-based beats; scene design is for causal narrative.
