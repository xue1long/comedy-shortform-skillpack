# Dialogue Listening — Jot + Crowd
## R — Reading
Dialogue craft begins in the ear, not on the page. Chiarella's method: (1) **Jot** — capture spoken fragments with a three-word context tag (who/where/mood). (2) **Crowd** — physically or socially move close enough to overhear real speech; non-physical crowding also works (eavesdrop via media). (3) **Tune in to catch, tune out to select** — record everything, then choose. Exercises: record yourself for a full day; transcribe a stranger's café conversation.
## I — Interpretation
Real speech is interrupted, elliptical, self-contradictory — the opposite of written dialogue. Writers who don't listen produce "book dialogue": complete sentences, consistent voice, every question answered. Jotting captures the *shape* of speech (fragments, false starts, rhythm) rather than content.
## A1 — Application
For comedy shortform: listen for mismatched registers (formal words in casual contexts), repeated pet phrases, and the gap between what people say and what they do — these are free comic material.
## A2 — Trigger signals
- "My dialogue sounds fake / written"
- "How do I make characters sound different?"
- "Where do I get natural speech rhythms?"
## E — Executable steps
1. Pick a real setting (office, subway, family dinner).
2. For one hour, jot fragments — each line: actual words + 3-word context.
3. Mark fragments with mismatched register or withheld information.
4. Convert 2–3 fragments into a two-line exchange.
5. Read aloud; cut any line that sounds "written."
## B — Boundary / common failures
- **Trap**: jotting meaning instead of sound — paraphrase loses the rhythm.
- **Trap**: crowding to confirm a character you already invented — you hear what you expect.
- **Not** for plot/structure work; this is raw-material collection only.
